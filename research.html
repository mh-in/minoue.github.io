Research

My research asks how genetic diversity in spore-dispersed organisms is structured across space and time, and how life-history traits — dispersal, establishment, and reproduction — shape these patterns. I address this question within the framework of phylogeography and biogeography, matching the analytical resolution to the study system: lineage-level phylogeographic and population-genetic analyses in club mosses (genus Diphasiastrum), and community-level analyses of multi-species assemblages in slime moulds (Myxomycetes).

Although the two groups differ entirely in phylogeny and life history, I ask the same question in both: how far do spore-dispersed organisms spread, and where do they diverge?

Current focus

Slime moulds (Myxomycetes)

Nivicolous myxomycetes fruit near melting snowbanks in the mountains. Combining DNA barcoding (18S rDNA) of field-collected fruiting bodies with climate and snow data, I study how snow conditions, climate, and geographic distance shape their communities and genetic diversity — currently across 11 mountain ranges of Eurasia. Our results point to dispersal limitation and regional endemism rather than unlimited gene flow.

Club mosses (Diphasiastrum, Lycopodiaceae)

In the flat-branched club mosses, hybridization and reticulate evolution are widespread. Using chloroplast and nuclear markers, flow cytometry, and multilocus genotyping (MIG-seq), I study range-wide genetic structure across the Northern Hemisphere, the origins of hybrids and polyploids, and the direction of hybridization — from Central Europe to East Asia.

Future directions

Building on my current work, I plan to pursue two directions:

  • Club mosses — why does reticulate evolution proceed differently from region to region? By comparing hybrid systems of Diphasiastrum across the Northern Hemisphere, I want to understand whether the direction and frequency of hybridization are set by the local frequencies of the parental species, by mating systems and genome compatibility, by disturbance history, or by a combination of these.
  • Slime moulds — linking environment and distribution. I plan to build a barcode-based reference database of myxomycetes and to extend the approach of our Eurasian study — combining barcode-based occurrence data with climate and other environmental data — to further regions, to understand how environment and dispersal together shape where myxomycetes occur.

Methods at a glance

DNA barcoding (18S rDNA) · Sanger sequencing of chloroplast and nuclear markers (cp-SSR, RPB2) · multilocus genotyping (MIG-seq, DOP) · flow cytometry · environmental data (temperature loggers, satellite data) · statistical analysis in R
